\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

On-policy reinforcement learning for contact-rich manipulation is limited in
practice by the amount of interaction it requires. Two families of techniques aim
to reduce this cost: reward shaping, which makes each sampled transition more
informative, and structured training, which orders or generates tasks through a
curriculum. This thesis compares the two on a single multi-objective task: planar
pushing of a T-shaped block by a simulated Universal Robots UR5e arm in NVIDIA
Isaac Lab, under a fixed compute budget and success criterion. The mechanics of
pushing couple translation and rotation, so a goal that specifies both a target
position and orientation cannot be decomposed into independent sub-problems. This
makes the task a stringent test of any method that treats the two objectives
separately. \\

Five models are compared under an identical protocol of 30 held-out scenes. These
are a single-agent baseline with a hand-tuned improvement reward, a single-agent
model with a potential-based reward, a forced position-to-orientation curriculum,
and two asymmetric self-play variants that differ only in how the goal-proposing
agent is rewarded. Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) uses bounded exponential
potentials over position and orientation, together with a unified Special
Euclidean group $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ distance. It reaches an 80.0\,\% scene success
rate at 528 parallel environments, matching the improvement reward at 2{,}048
environments and improving combined-objective success by 35 percentage points.
Neither the forced curriculum nor either self-play variant beats the shaped
single-agent model. The curriculum is statistically equivalent to it, while the
self-play variants reach at most 16.7\,\%. A cross-environment comparison in a
second simulator shows that the value of the curriculum depends on the per-update
batch size. \\

A per-axis analysis explains the self-play failure. Self-play learns the individual
position and orientation skills, each to roughly 50\,\%, but combines them at only
about 10\,\%, roughly 2.5 times below what independent skills would predict. The
goal-proposing agent, meanwhile, remains a reliable source of valid goals. The
failure is therefore one of skill composition under a non-stationary goal
distribution, not of skill acquisition. For this class of contact-rich
multi-objective task, the results indicate a clear priority: investment in
principled reward design produces a larger and more reliable improvement than
investment in structured training dynamics.
